What are requirements?
A property a product must have to provide value to stakeholders.
Wiegers & Beatty, Software Requirements

Before any work begins, someone has to figure out what we are building and why. Requirements turn a vague idea into clear goals so the client, designers, developers, and creatives are all building the same thing.

When requirements go wrong
Over budgetRework costs hours we planned for something else.
Behind scheduleWe find the real need late, then rebuild.
The wrong thingIt works perfectly and solves nothing.
Are we building the system right?Code quality
Are we building the right system?Requirements

Excellent code can faithfully implement the wrong requirement.

From our own team leads

Questions our leads already ask at intake. Some teams are still blank.

Project Management

  • When does it need to be completed?
  • Who is the best person for our PM to communicate with?

Design

  • Does this need to follow BSU brand guidelines? How “officially BSU” is this?
  • How should users feel when they visit the site?
  • What device will the average user use most?

Comms & Marketing

  • Who is your target audience?
  • What is your goal for this project?
  • What should people take away from it?
  • How formal is the tone?
  • If interviews are involved, who should we interview?

Accessibility

  • Is the product meant for the public?
  • Is it part of classroom or educational content?
  • What platforms will it be on?
  • Are there existing alternatives to the product?

User Experience

What would you ask?

  • Who uses this today, and can we talk to them?
  • What does someone do right before and after using it?

Development

What would you ask?

  • Does this connect to an existing system or login?
  • Who keeps it running after launch?

Fabrication

What would you ask?

  • Where will the piece live, and how will people touch or use it?
  • Any size, material, or safety limits?

Video & Media

What would you ask?

  • Where will this be watched, and how long can it run?
  • Who appears on camera, and do we have permission?

The shape of a good interview

Your goal is understanding, not getting through your list.

Open

Build rapport. Say who you are and why you're here.

“Thanks for meeting. I'm on the dev team, and I'll be helping build this.”

15 of 28 opened badly

Explore

Hear their story and how things work today.

“Walk me through the last time this happened.”

Probe

Follow the interesting answer.

“Usually? What happens when it doesn't?”

“Can you give me an example?”

11 of 28 never probed

Confirm

Summarize back what you heard.

“Let me make sure I got this right…”

19 of 28 ended without this

Close

Ask what you missed and who else to talk to.

“What should I have asked that I didn't?”

“Who else should we talk to?”

Flow from CS439 Lecture 5, Interviews and Surveys. Statistics from Bano et al., 2018.

Question toolkit

Small changes, very different answers.

Closed → Open“Do you use the search filter?”→“Walk me through the last time you tried to find something on the site.”
Leading → Neutral“Don't you think a faster checkout would help?”→“How do you feel about the checkout process today?”
Solution → Need“So you want a chatbot?”→“What problem would a chatbot solve for you? When does it happen?”
Phrases that keep it safe and respectful
Asking permission“Can I ask a clarifying question?”
Not promising“Help me understand how that works.”
Checking yourself“I want to make sure I heard that right…”
Avoiding jargon“When you say ‘portal,’ what do you mean?”
Examples adapted from CS439 Lecture 4, Requirements Elicitation.

Write it down

Write down the question, not just the answer.

What we heard
Follow up / unclear

Google Form, then Maria copies it to a spreadsheet.

What happens when an event is full?

Usually? Is there a waitlist sometimes?
How will you know this worked?
Fewer no-shows. They don't track it yet.
Assumption: we can measure no-shows.
In an email chain: number your questions so none get buried.
In a meeting: one person asks, one person writes. Then swap.
After: send the client a short summary. It catches what you misheard.
From the study: one team's notes

Q: “What do you think is the better performance?”

Recorded answer: the owner hopes the new system can support online operations, such as request tracking.

A vague question got a vague answer, and it went straight into the notes. The answer had nothing to do with performance.

Teams that asked vague questions or skipped key ones also wrote poor notes. Teams that did well wrote good ones.

On your next project

Try three things.

1

Draft three questions before the meeting.

At least one “Walk me through…” question. Ask for a project summary if you don't have one.

2

Ask one clarifying question.

Just one. “Help me understand…” is always allowed, whatever team you're on.

3

Write it down, then summarize back.

Note the question and the answer. End by confirming what you heard.
